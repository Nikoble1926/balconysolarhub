Is plug-in solar legal in Great Britain? (2026)

Law made 16 July 2026 — in force since 27 August 2026   SI 2026 No. 848   Updated: 24 September 2026

Last verified: ENA Type Test Register, 24 September 2026 at 23:07. SI 2026/848 on legislation.gov.uk was last re-read on 9 September 2026 and is unchanged since; we did not re-read it for this update

Quick answer: In Great Britain, plug-in solar has been legal since 27 August 2026. That is the day SI 2026 No. 848 came into force, for a solar device with a maximum rated AC output not exceeding 800W that complies with the Interim Product Specification v2 — full detail on the new law. Before that date, and for any device outside that definition after it, the compliant route is an 800W hardwired install by a CPS-registered electrician with a G98 Connect & Notify to your DNO — which is what BS 7671 Amendment 4 (ratified 16 March 2026, in force 15 April 2026) enabled when it reclassified ≤800W microgeneration as a portable appliance. Amendment 4 did not by itself create true plug-and-play DIY. No scheme certifies against IPS v2 itself; the ENA register’s own “Compliant” status was carried by seventy-one of seventy-nine plug-in solar entries in our 24 September 2026 reading — what that does and does not mean. Ask the retailer what they will put in writing.
Correction (9 September 2026): an earlier version of this page said plug-in solar “becomes legal on 27 August 2026”. That date has passed. We re-read SI 2026/848 as made on 9 September 2026 — regulation 1(2), “come into force on 27th August 2026” — and moved this page to the past tense. July 2026: the law is made — in force since 27 August 2026

On 16 June 2026 the government (DESNZ) formally proposed legalising plug-in (balcony) solar and opened a public consultation, which closed on 30 June 2026 at 23:59. The regulations were then made on 16 July 2026 and laid before Parliament on 17 July 2026: The Plugs and Sockets etc. (Safety) Regulations 1994 and Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations 2002 (Amendment) Regulations 2026, SI 2026 No. 848. They came into force on 27 August 2026 — that is the day the plug-in route opened. Before that date the compliant route was a hardwired install by a CPS-registered electrician plus a G98 notification, and that route remains available.

The instrument amends both the Plugs and Sockets etc. (Safety) Regulations 1994 (so a notified body can approve a standard plug intended for a plug-in microgenerator) and the Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations 2002 (so such a device must not be installed or operated unless it complies with the specification). The controlling document is the Plug-in Solar Device Interim Product Specification, version 2, published 16 July 2026, with an enduring BSI standard to follow.

Where this applies. The instrument sets out its own extent, and the two paragraphs do different things: “(3) Regulations 1 and 2 extend to England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. (4) Regulation 3 extends to England and Wales and Scotland.” Regulation 3 is the one that amends the Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations 2002 — the change that makes installing and operating a plug-in microgenerator lawful on the new route. It extends to England, Wales and Scotland. Regulation 2, the plug-approval route, extends to Northern Ireland as well. What otherwise applies in Northern Ireland rests on separate legislation that this instrument does not touch, and we are not going to guess at it.

The law defines a “plug-in microgenerator” in five limbs, and the 800W cap is only one of them. The definition appears twice in the instrument — at regulation 2(3), into regulation 8 of the Plugs and Sockets etc. (Safety) Regulations 1994, and at regulation 3(2), into regulation 1(5) of ESQCR 2002 — with identical wording in both places. Verbatim:

“plug-in microgenerator” means a source of energy which—

  1. generates electricity from the direct conversion of sunlight into electricity;
  2. has a maximum rated alternating current output not exceeding 800 watts;
  3. is intended for connection to a low voltage consumer’s installation by means of a standard plug and socket;
  4. is designed to operate in parallel with a distributor’s network; and
  5. is not designed to import electrical energy from a low voltage consumer’s installation for the purpose of storing energy for later supply, other than for control or auxiliary functions permitted by the Plug-in Solar Device Interim Product Specification;

There is still no certification scheme against the Interim Product Specification itself — what the register gives is its own “Compliant” status, and what that does and does not mean is set out below. Before buying for the plug-in route, check the ENA Type Test Register yourself — it is public and needs no login. Filter Type to “Plug-in Solar”. Read on 24 September 2026 at 23:07 it listed seventy-nine plug-in solar entries. Seventy-one of them carried the status “Compliant”, from nineteen manufacturers — City Plumbing Supplies (thirteen), Perlight (thirteen), UKSOL Ltd (seven), PatioSun (six), Zendure (six), four from Modular Solar, three from TSUNESS, two each from APsystems, EcoFlow, HDM Solar, InstaGroup, Skyworth, The Solar Centre and Thunder Energy, and one each from Octopus Energy, PowerXpress, Segen, The Impact Crowd and VALE. The other eight — three from Sigenergy, and one each from InstaGroup, Plugsol, Voltacon, The Solar Home and Longshen — were “Non-compliant”. One manufacturer, InstaGroup, appears on both sides of the line. Compliant here is the register’s own assessment, that network operators will accept the equipment. It is not the same thing as certification to the Interim Product Specification v2, and we have not verified that any of these kits carries that certification. The register’s own wording for each status is quoted in full further down this page. There is no third-party certification against the specification itself, and IPS v2 §7 says a device merely submitted for registration does not count. Statuses change — check the register rather than trusting this page.

Why the plug needed its own route. Regulation 2(2) inserts a new paragraph (3A) into the 1994 Plugs and Sockets Regulations so that a notified body can approve a plug type which does not conform to BS 1363 — because “BS 1363 contains a provision which prohibits or restricts the use of the plug for the connection of an electricity-generating device to a socket-outlet”. Coverage saying a plug-in kit “must be BS 1363” has it backwards.

The requirement sits inside the definition, so a device that does not meet all five limbs never reaches the new route at all. For a worked example on two products people are already buying, see EcoFlow STREAM vs Anker SOLIX.

Detailed figures (voltage, current, DC panel wattage, fuse rating, devices per household) sit in the Interim Product Specification v2, not in the regulations — so we do not quote numbers here that the legislation itself does not state.

What Amendment 4 actually changes

Amendment 4 to the IET’s 18th Edition Wiring Regulations (BS 7671) does one specific, narrow thing: it reclassifies a compliant microgeneration unit up to 800W as a portable appliance, rather than a permanent generation installation requiring Part P notification and a full Electrical Installation Certificate.

For that reclassification to apply, the unit must meet all of:

  • 800W AC inverter output cap (not panel watts — you can have higher-rated panels behind a clipping inverter).
  • Single-phase connection.
  • Type-approved anti-islanding inverter — the inverter must disconnect within ~0.2 seconds when grid voltage drops, so it cannot energise a dead grid (protects line workers).
  • RCD protection on the final circuit it connects to.

What Amendment 4 does not do: create a product standard for off-the-shelf plug-in kits. That part is delivered by SI 2026 No. 848 together with the Interim Product Specification v2, in force 27 August 2026, with an enduring BSI standard to follow.

Where you may not install one

This is the question that comes before “which kit”, and almost nobody asks it. The Interim Product Specification v2 — the document the regulations name — puts building and surface restrictions on the plug-in route. Section 5.8, verbatim:

“The manufacturer shall identify any planning restrictions on installation surfaces or building types applicable to the product. Installations shall not be permitted on: • aluminium composite material (ACM) or metal composite material (MCM) cladding systems; • high pressure laminate (HPL) cladding systems; • timber cladding systems; • timber balconies; • buildings that are subject to external wall remediation works, building safety remediation works or equivalent restrictions relating to external wall fire safety.”

A timber balcony is out. So is a building under external wall remediation — which, after Grenfell, is a great many blocks of flats. This is written as a duty on the manufacturer to identify and declare, expressed as a restriction on where the product may be installed. It is not us telling you what is legal. But if your balcony is timber, or your block is mid-remediation, the plug-in route is not the one to plan around.

One device per household, not per circuit. Section 1, verbatim: “NOTE: While this specification permits one device per final circuit, GB network connection requirements also apply. As of publication, current network requirements Engineering Recommendation G98 Issue 2 Amendment 1 2026 restrict this to one device per household, which applies unless and until that is amended.” The limit comes from G98, not from the specification, and the note says it can change.

No adaptors, and the plug is the manufacturer’s. Section 6.2.3: “The use of multi-way adaptors, travel adaptors, RCD adaptors and plug convertors shall not be permitted.” Section 6.2.3.2 says what the plug must be: “Plug-in solar intended for connection to UK sockets shall use plugs designed to BS 1363-1”, with “partially insulated pins to prevent access to live conductive parts” and a “non-rewireable moulded plug fitted with a BS 1362 fuse not exceeding 5A”. So it is still a BS 1363 plug. The approval route in the instrument exists because BS 1363 restricts using a plug to connect a generating device to a socket — not because the plug should be something else.

Planning law reaches the same answer for wood, separately. A second instrument in force the same day — SI 2026 No. 896, the planning order — excludes plug-in solar on a wooden fence, gate or enclosure, on a wooden wall or balcony, and on any part of a house clad in timber. Two different routes, the same result. See planning rules for plug-in solar. The full buyer’s checklist pulls together the product requirements that decide whether a kit qualifies.

The voltage problem nobody mentions

Before blaming a kit that keeps dropping out, check the voltage it is being asked to work with. This is a recognised issue in the industry with an open reform attached to it, and almost none of the buying guides mention it.

The range the law allows. The Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations 2002 set it in regulation 27. Regulation 27(2): “the voltage declared in respect of a low voltage supply shall be 230 volts between the phase and neutral conductors at the supply terminals”. Regulation 27(3)(b): “in the case of a low voltage supply, a variation not exceeding 10 per cent above or 6 per cent below the declared voltage at the declared frequency”. That puts the top of the legal range at 253 V and the bottom at 216 V.

What the network operators say about it. The Energy Networks Association, the body for the distribution network operators, on its Statutory Voltage Limits programme page: “UK mains electricity operates at a nominal voltage of 230V, and can operate within limits of 253V and 216V set by the Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations (ESQCR).” And on why that range is under pressure: “The connection of low carbon technologies (LCTs), such as domestic solar generation and electric vehicle chargers, has led to an increasing challenge in maintaining the voltage range within those specified in the ESQCR. This can cause over voltage, which can cause household and commercial equipment, including EV chargers, to shut down when protective limits are reached.”

Read that carefully for what it does not say. Over voltage can cause equipment to shut down — not that it does, and not everywhere. And domestic solar generation is named among the things putting pressure on the range, not among the things suffering from it.

What the specification expects a device to cope with. Interim Product Specification v2 carries the same number: it applies to plug-in solar “with a rated voltage of up to 253 V (AC)”, and it requires that “The inverter shall be tested to determine its maximum apparent power output at supply voltages of 0.94 Un, 1 Un and 1.1 Un, where Un is the nominal supply voltage (230V)”. 1.1 × 230 is 253. A device built to the specification is expected to still be working at the top of the legal range.

The reform. ENA says network operators are “proposing to amend the lower voltage limit in ESQCR from 216V to 207V”. The 253 V ceiling is not part of that proposal. Lowering the floor gives operators room to run the whole network further below the ceiling. ENA says the consultation has closed and that “we are now reviewing those responses and developing our evidence case document” before taking options to the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero. Nothing in the regulations has changed.

What to do with it. If an inverter keeps disconnecting in an area with a lot of rooftop solar, a faulty kit is not the only explanation and may not be the likeliest. The thing to establish first is what the supply voltage actually is at the socket, measured across a day rather than at one moment. Your distribution network operator can tell you what it records, and a qualified electrician can log it. If it sits near the top of the range, that is a conversation with the network operator rather than with the retailer. What a G98-certified unit is actually required to survive at 253 V, and where an EU device stops, is set out separately.

Voltage limits read from the Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations 2002, regulation 27, on legislation.gov.uk. Network operator wording read from the Energy Networks Association Statutory Voltage Limits programme page on 6 August 2026.

How to check a device yourself

This is the part you do not need us for. IPS v2 §7 requires manufacturers to “register devices on the ENA Type Test Register… and obtain confirmation that the device has been assessed and identified as compliant prior to placing them on the market”. That register is public:

  1. Open connect-direct.energynetworks.org and go to the Live Device Register. No account is needed to search.
  2. Open Filters and set Type to “Plug-in Solar”.
  3. Read the Compliance Status column for the device you are considering.
  4. Ask the retailer for the System Reference so you can look up that exact entry rather than a similar model name.

What each route requires, and from when

Broken down by install path, with the date each one applies from.

✅ 800W hardwired install by a CPS-registered electrician

This route is available now and stays available after 27 August 2026. The electrician fits the microinverter, isolator and protection in line with Amendment 4, certifies under Part P, and you (or they) file a G98 Connect & Notify with your DNO. Typical professional installation cost: £300–£600 on top of kit price.

❗ True plug-and-play DIY kit

This route opened on 27 August 2026, when SI 2026 No. 848 came into force. It requires the device to meet all five limbs of the definition above and to comply with the Interim Product Specification v2. There is no certification scheme against that specification itself; what the register gives is its own “Compliant” status, carried by seventy-one plug-in solar entries in our 24 September 2026 reading — what that does and does not mean. A CE or EN mark is not the same thing: it certifies against European standards, not against the specification the regulations name. Until a product states that it meets IPS v2, you are inferring compliance rather than relying on a UK-stamped one — so ask the retailer to confirm it before you buy.

❌ “Just plug it in” with no DNO notification, no RCD, no anti-islanding inverter

Not legal. The ≤800W “portable appliance” classification only applies when the four safety conditions above are met and the DNO has been notified via G98. Skipping any of those puts you outside Amendment 4’s coverage.

FAQs

Do I need planning permission for balcony solar in the UK?
For most flats: no. Microgeneration up to 800W on a balcony or external wall does not normally require planning permission, but listed buildings and some conservation areas have additional restrictions. Always check with your local planning authority and, for a flat, your leasehold/freeholder.
Do I get paid for exported electricity (SEG)?
Generally no, in practice. The Smart Export Guarantee (SEG) requires an MCS-certified install and a smart meter, which most plug-in balcony installs do not meet. Treat all savings as self-consumption only — every kWh your kit makes that your appliances use is a kWh you don’t pay your supplier for, at ~27–28p/kWh under the current price cap.
What if I rent?
You need landlord permission. Amendment 4 doesn’t override your tenancy agreement. A polite written request showing the kit is portable, Amendment 4-compliant, and removable without damage is the usual route. Many landlords say yes.
What if my flat is leasehold?
Check your lease for clauses about altering the external appearance of the building or attaching anything to the structure. Even a non-permanent install may need freeholder consent. This is the most common UK gotcha.
How is this different from Germany?
Germany legalised 800W “Balkonkraftwerk” plug-in solar in 2024 with a product standard (VDE-AR-N 4105) already in place — DIY installs took off immediately. The UK rule change in Amendment 4 is equivalent in spirit, but the product-standard piece landed with SI 2026 No. 848 and the Interim Product Specification v2 on 27 August 2026.
